Deuteronomy 28 Covenant Lawsuit AD 70 Audience Relevance

Deuteronomy 28 and the Judgment of Jerusalem

The fall of the city, the Second Temple, and the old commonwealth in AD 70 — read as the historical execution of the covenant curses, not as a bare political defeat.

Luke 21:20–22 (WEB)

“But when you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then know that its desolation is at hand. Then let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains. Let those who are in the middle of her depart. Let those who are in the country not enter therein. For these are days of vengeance, that all things which are written may be fulfilled.”

1. A lawsuit, not only a siege

In the New Testament’s own frame, the judgment of Jerusalem is the catastrophic destruction of the city, the Second Temple, and the Jewish commonwealth by the Roman legions under Titus in AD 70. For the generation Jesus addressed, that event was not received merely as a military tragedy. It was the public close of a covenantal lawsuit — the rib Yahweh brings against a people who have broken the terms they swore at Sinai.

The prophets already knew this courtroom. When Israel breached the covenant, the indictment was not invented on the spot. Yahweh invoked the sanctions already written in the Torah. Deuteronomy 28 is that sanction list: blessings for loyalty in verses 1–14, and the escalating curses — the tokhechah — for unfaithfulness in verses 15–68. AD 70 is the point in history where those written curses meet the generation Jesus named.

The first-century historian Flavius Josephus, a priest who survived the war and wrote The Jewish War for a Roman audience, is the principal eyewitness record of the siege. He is not a Christian commentator. He is a witness to the city, the factions, the famine, and the captivity. Read beside Deuteronomy 28, his account is the historical face of a text Israel already possessed.

2. Deuteronomy 28 as the rulebook

The Mosaic covenant was not a vague religious mood. It was a sworn administration with published terms. Obedience inside that administration brought the blessings of the land — rain, harvest, security, and the fear of enemies falling away. Breach brought the reverse: disease, drought, siege, exile, and the horror of a people eating the fruit of their own body inside a city that would not open.

Those curses are not random punishments. They reverse the exodus. The nation that was brought out of Egypt with a mighty hand is threatened with a return to Egypt in ships, offering themselves as slaves with no buyer (Deuteronomy 28:68). The people who trusted in high walls are told those walls will come down. The table of the covenant meal becomes the table of famine. The land that was gift becomes the scene of scattering “from one end of the earth to the other” (Deuteronomy 28:64).

Jesus does not set this book aside. He tells the disciples that the armies around Jerusalem are the signal that desolation has come near, “for these are days of vengeance, that all things which are written may be fulfilled” (Luke 21:22). “All that is written” includes the sanction chapter Moses left on the table. The Olivet discourse is not a new curse list. It is the announcement that the old list has reached its generation.

Audience relevance. The “you” of Deuteronomy 28 is Israel under the Mosaic oath. The “you” of Matthew 23 and Luke 21 is the generation standing in the temple courts — “this generation” (Matthew 23:36; 24:34). The curses are not a transferable threat aimed at the church, nor a program for a later ethnic politics. They land on the administration that kept the house while rejecting the Heir.

3. The nation from afar, and the sign of the eagle

“Yahweh will bring a nation against you from far, from the end of the earth, as the eagle flies: a nation whose language you will not understand, a nation of fierce facial expressions, that will not respect the elderly, nor show favor to the young.”

Deuteronomy 28:49–50 (WEB)

Rome is that distant nation relative to Judea — an empire whose reach ran to the end of the known earth, whose legions and auxiliaries were drawn from provinces far from Jerusalem, and whose public language was not the Aramaic and Hebrew of the land. The primary standard of a Roman legion was the aquila, the eagle. Deuteronomy had already chosen the simile: a nation swooping “as the eagle flies.”

The character of the nation matches the text’s severity. Josephus records a siege in which age was no shield. The aged and the infirm were cut down with the rest; priests died at the altar; infants were not spared by the collapse of a city whose defenders had already turned on one another. The Torah did not promise a gentlemanly war if the covenant was broken. It promised a nation that would not respect the old or favor the young.

The prophecy

A far nation, an eagle’s swoop, a foreign tongue, no mercy to old or young (Deut 28:49–50).

The siege

Rome, the legionary eagle, Latin over Aramaic, and a sack that did not sort the city by age.

4. The walls they trusted

“They will besiege you in all your gates until your high and fortified walls come down, in which you trusted, throughout all your land.”

Deuteronomy 28:52 (WEB)

Jerusalem’s confidence was architectural. The First, Second, and Third Walls, with the Antonia fortress overlooking the temple, were treated as nearly impregnable. Jesus had already contradicted that confidence: not one stone of the temple buildings would be left on another (Matthew 24:2). Deuteronomy had said the same thing in older words. The high walls “in which you trusted” would come down.

Titus did not take the city in a day. Siege ramps, towers, catapults, and battering rams worked wall by wall — the outer wall, the second wall, the Antonia, then the temple courts, then the upper city. What the generation trusted as permanent was methodically reduced. The sanctuary that had been the center of the Levitical world was burned in AD 70 and the complex razed. Trust in stone is the point of the curse. The walls were real. They were not the covenant.

5. Famine, faction, and the tender woman

“You will eat the fruit of your own body, the flesh of your sons and of your daughters… The tender and delicate woman among you… her eye will be evil toward the husband of her bosom… and toward her children whom she bears; for she will eat them secretly for lack of all things in the siege and in the distress.”

Deuteronomy 28:53, 56–57 (WEB)

The famine of AD 70 was not only the work of the Roman ring. Inside the walls, rival factions — Zealot bands, the Sicarii, the forces of John of Gischala and Simon bar Giora — fought a civil war while the legions built ramps. Josephus records that the factions burned the city’s grain stores to force one another into the open. The food that could have carried the population through the siege was destroyed by the men who claimed to be defending it.

The curse then takes a form Moses had already written, and that Jerusalem had seen once before in the Babylonian siege (Lamentations 2:20; 4:10; 2 Kings 6:28–29). Josephus tells of Mary, daughter of Eleazar, from the village of Bethezuba beyond the Jordan — a woman of birth and wealth who had fled into the city. In the extremity of the famine she killed her infant son, roasted him, and ate part of the body, hiding the rest (Jewish War 6.201–213). Josephus shapes the scene so that a reader of the Torah cannot miss it: the tender and refined woman, the secret meal, the siege and the distress. He is describing the text of Deuteronomy 28:56–57 in a street of the besieged city.

This is not included to horrify for its own sake. It is the sanction the covenant itself published, arriving in the generation that filled up the measure of blood from Abel to Zechariah (Matthew 23:35–36).

6. Pestilence, and the unburied dead

“Yahweh will make the pestilence cling to you… Your dead bodies will be food to all birds of the sky, and to the animals of the earth; and there will be no one to frighten them away.”

Deuteronomy 28:21, 26 (WEB)

Titus closed the city at the season of Passover, the Feast of Unleavened Bread. Pilgrims from the land were inside the walls when the ring shut. Josephus’s tally — 1,100,000 dead through the siege, a figure widely regarded as high, and 97,000 taken captive through the war — is his own accounting, built in part on the Passover crowds (Jewish War 6.420). The number can be debated. The condition he describes cannot: overcrowding, then pestilence, then a famine that killed faster than disease.

Burial collapsed with the city. Josephus describes bodies carried to the gates and thrown into the valleys because the living could no longer bury the dead, and because the factions would not pause for graves. Deuteronomy 28:26 had already assigned the unburied corpse to the birds and the beasts. The Valley of Hinnom — Gehenna, the dump and the old burning-place on the city’s south — became the image the prophets had used for a judgment that does not tidy itself. Jesus had warned of Gehenna in the same breath as the blood-guilt of that generation. The valleys around the wall were the visible form of the warning.

7. The exodus reversed

“Yahweh will scatter you among all peoples, from one end of the earth to the other… Yahweh will bring you into Egypt again with ships… There you will offer yourselves to your enemies for male and female slaves, and there will be no buyer.”

Deuteronomy 28:64, 68 (WEB)

The exodus was the founding mercy of the old covenant: out of the house of bondage, never to return that way again. Deuteronomy 28:68 undoes the mercy in a single sentence. Josephus records the undoing. Of the survivors, those over seventeen were put in bonds and sent to the works in Egypt; many were distributed to the provinces to die in the theaters by sword and beast; those under seventeen were sold (Jewish War 6.418). Ninety-seven thousand, on his count, were carried captive in the course of the war.

The last line of the curse — “there will be no buyer” — is the market at the end of the story. A captive people so numerous that sale itself failed as a solution: the young were priced into a glutted trade, and the rest were not purchased into households but spent in mines and arenas. The nation that had come out of Egypt under Moses was, in the persons of its captives, sent back toward Egypt in chains. Scattering “among all peoples” was no longer a threat on a scroll. It was the disposition of the prisoners.

8. Jesus names the same event

The apostolic writings do not leave the identification to later inference. Jesus attaches the written curses to a generation and a city.

  • This generation. “Most certainly I tell you, all these things will come upon this generation” (Matthew 23:36). The blood from Abel to Zechariah is charged to the house that would not receive the prophets — and then would not receive the Son.
  • The house left desolate. “Behold, your house is left to you desolate” (Matthew 23:38). The temple is no longer “my Father’s house” in the sense of an occupied administration. It is theirs, and it is empty of the Presence they claimed to guard.
  • Days of vengeance. Luke 21:22 is explicit. The armies, the desolation, and the flight to the mountains are “days of vengeance, that all things which are written may be fulfilled.” Daniel’s abomination, Moses’ curses, and the prophets’ dirges converge on one siege.
  • Not one stone. The disciples’ admiration of the buildings is answered with a demolition notice (Matthew 24:1–2). The Levitical system required that house: sacrifice, genealogical priesthood, the localized sanctuary. When the house fell, the administration that could only function there fell with it.

The cross and the fall of the city are not rivals. The cross bears the curse for those who are in Christ (Galatians 3:13). AD 70 is the public end of the curse-bearing administration that refused Him. Paul can say the sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the Law (1 Corinthians 15:56). When that Law-system loses its temple, its priesthood, and its city, the prosecutor loses the court in which that administration operated.

9. What this is — and is not

It is: the historical execution of Deuteronomy 28 upon the Old Covenant order in the generation Jesus addressed; the desolation of a house; the end of sacrifice in that sanctuary; the vindication of the Son against the stewards who rejected Him; the close of the age whose heaven and earth were the covenant world centered on Jerusalem.

It is not: a warrant for hostility toward Jewish heritage; a claim that every descendant of Abraham was swept away (a remnant was saved, and the dividing wall came down); a statement that Gentiles in Christ ever stood under Sinai’s lawsuit; or a denial that physical mortality remains the created condition of Adam’s race. What ended was the covenantal death — exile, condemnation, the ministry of the Law — not the return of the body to dust.

The judgment distinguishes in order to unite. The remnant according to the election of grace, and the nations brought near by the blood of Christ, become one new man (Ephesians 2:14–16). The kingdom is not confiscated from Abraham. It is given, as Jesus said, to a people producing its fruit (Matthew 21:43) — a people defined by faith, not by the genealogy the temple stones had guarded.

10. New Creation reality

If you are in Christ, Deuteronomy 28 is not your summons. The curse as condemning covenant status was borne by the Son. “There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus” (Romans 8:1). You are not invited back under Moses to complete a sentence the cross already answered, and you are not waiting for a second edition of Jerusalem’s siege to know whether the old order has ended.

The blessings of Abraham come to the nations in Christ Jesus, so that we receive the promise of the Spirit through faith (Galatians 3:14). That is the Eighth Day side of the same story. The house fell. The city of the living God remains — Mount Zion, already come to (Hebrews 12:22). The unshakable kingdom is the ground under the feet of those in the Last Adam, not a world still pending after history ends.

11. Key takeaways

  • One rulebook. Deuteronomy 28 is the sanction chapter of the Mosaic oath. AD 70 is that chapter in the streets of Jerusalem.
  • One generation. Jesus ties the blood-guilt, the desolate house, and the days of vengeance to “this generation,” and Luke 21 says the siege fulfills what is written.
  • Point by point. Eagle and foreign nation, fallen walls, factional famine, the woman of Bethezuba, pestilence, unburied dead, and the return toward Egypt in bonds — Josephus narrates what Moses listed.
  • Two horizons of curse. Redeemed from the curse in Christ; the old house bears the historical curse it chose. These are two sides of one transition.
  • One new man. The fall of the sanctuary removes the dividing wall’s public furniture. What remains is the New Covenant people, Jew and Gentile, in the Son.
