Syria Palaestina Is Nearly 2,000 Years Old: The Old Covenant Ended, There Is No Jew or Gentile, and the Land Was Never a Bloodline Prize

From the curse clause of Deuteronomy 28 to the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, Hadrian’s renaming of the province, and the New Creation: the covenant story, and what it means for the land.

The name Syria Palaestina has marked this region for almost 1,900 years, since the Emperor Hadrian imposed it after crushing the Bar Kokhba revolt around AD 135. That is documented imperial history, not a modern invention. But the decisive event came sixty-five years earlier, and it was not a Roman one. It was the close of an age. To see why that matters for the land, for Jewish people today, and for the kingdom itself, we have to start where Scripture starts: with a covenant that came with a curse clause.

1. A covenant with sanctions

Israel did not receive the land as an unconditional deed. It received it under a covenant, and covenants in the ancient world came with terms, witnesses, and sanctions. Deuteronomy 28 is the sanctions section. Obey, and there is blessing: rain, harvest, fertility, safety from enemies. Break the covenant, and every blessing reverses into curse: failed crops, disease, defeat, siege, and finally exile (Deut 28:15-68).

Three things are worth noticing.

  • The curse section is about six times longer than the blessing section. Moses was not being decorative. He was warning.
  • The curse is tied to the land. The worst outcome was not poverty but removal: “You shall be plucked off the land” and “the Lord will scatter you among all peoples” (Deut 28:63-64).
  • The land itself was never Israel’s to own outright. “The land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me” (Lev 25:23).

So the land came as a covenant gift, held on covenant conditions. The Babylonian exile was the curse landing once already. When the exiles returned, Nehemiah’s prayer shows they did not feel the curse had lifted: “Behold, we are slaves this day... in the land that you gave to our fathers” (Neh 9:36). Israel remained under the covenant’s shadow, awaiting its resolution.

See also the full study: Deuteronomy 28 and the Judgment of Jerusalem.

2. The prophets were sent, and killed

God did not execute the sanctions without warning. He sent prophet after prophet to call the nation back. Scripture’s own summary is blunt: “They mocked the messengers of God, despised his words and scoffed at his prophets, until the wrath of the Lord rose against his people” (2 Chron 36:16). Stephen asked the council, “Which of the prophets did your fathers not persecute? And they killed those who announced beforehand the coming of the Righteous One” (Acts 7:52).

The pattern is mercy offered, mercy refused, and the messenger killed. It builds toward a final messenger.

3. Jesus arrives: freedom from the curse, and a call to repent

Jesus began in the synagogue at Nazareth reading Isaiah: “He has sent me to proclaim liberty to the captives... to set at liberty those who are oppressed” (Luke 4:18). His first public word, like John the Baptist’s before him, was a summons: “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand” (Matt 4:17).

John had already struck at the ground of national confidence: “Do not presume to say to yourselves, ‘We have Abraham as our father,’ for I tell you, God is able from these stones to raise up children for Abraham” (Matt 3:9). Descent was not the ticket. Paul later states the gospel’s answer to Deuteronomy 28: “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us” (Gal 3:13).

The offer was real: freedom from the curse, on the basis of turning to the Messiah. The question was whether the nation, and especially its leaders, would accept it.

4. The vineyard owner’s son (Matthew 21)

In the Temple courts, in the final week, Jesus confronted the establishment directly. He told of a landowner who planted a vineyard and leased it to tenants. When he sent servants to collect the fruit, the tenants beat one, killed another, and stoned a third. He sent more servants, and they were treated the same. Finally he sent his son: “They will respect my son.” But the tenants said, “This is the heir. Come, let’s kill him and have his inheritance” (Matt 21:33-39).

Jesus then asked the chief priests and elders what the owner would do. They answered against themselves: “He will put those wretches to a miserable death and let out the vineyard to other tenants who will give him the fruits in their seasons.” Jesus drove it home: “The kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits” (Matt 21:41-43). Matthew adds that the leaders knew he was speaking about them (21:45).

The story compresses the whole history: owner, vineyard, servants (the prophets), son (Jesus), tenants (the leadership), and a verdict. The charge is aimed at the tenants, the stewards of that generation, not at an entire people.

5. The woes (Matthew 23)

Matthew 21 runs straight into Matthew 23. Jesus pronounces seven woes on the scribes and Pharisees for hypocrisy, for shutting the door of the kingdom in people’s faces, and for building the tombs of the prophets while carrying on their fathers’ work. Then comes the charge: “Fill up, then, the measure of your fathers... so that on you may come all the righteous blood shed on earth, from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah... Truly, I say to you, all these things will come upon this generation” (Matt 23:32-36).

He ends in grief, not triumph: “O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often would I have gathered your children together... and you were not willing. See, your house is left to you desolate” (Matt 23:37-38). The Deuteronomy 28 curse was about to come due on that generation.

6. The Olivet Discourse: judgment, and a way out

As Jesus left the Temple, his disciples pointed out its buildings. He told them: “There will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down” (Matt 24:2). Then he tied the timing to his own hearers: “Truly, I say to you, this generation will not pass away until all these things take place” (Matt 24:34).

He also gave a way of escape. “When you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then know that its desolation has come near. Then let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains” (Luke 21:20-21). Salvation in that moment meant leaving the city, not defending it.

He framed the event with two earlier judgments: “As it was in the days of Noah... and as it was in the days of Lot” (Luke 17:26-29). In both, ordinary life carried on (eating, drinking, buying, building) until the day the righteous were taken out and judgment fell. Noah entered the ark, and Lot left Sodom. Then Jesus added: “Remember Lot’s wife” (Luke 17:32), a warning not to look back and cling to the doomed city. The pattern is the same each time. Those who heed the warning and leave are saved, and those who stay are caught in the judgment.

7. AD 70: the end of the age

In AD 66 the Jewish revolt against Rome began. In the spring of AD 70, at Passover season, Titus surrounded Jerusalem. The siege ended with the Temple burned and the city taken. The Jewish historian Josephus witnessed the war and recorded it. Early Christian tradition, preserved by Eusebius, says the Jerusalem church heeded the warning and fled across the Jordan to Pella.

With the Temple went the system built around it: sacrifice, priesthood, and the land-centered national order. The writer of Hebrews, composing before that moment, said of the first covenant: “In speaking of a new covenant, he makes the first one obsolete. And what is becoming obsolete and growing old is ready to vanish away” (Heb 8:13). Hebrews 10:9 puts it directly: “He does away with the first in order to establish the second.”

AD 70 was not merely a military defeat. It was the close of the Old Covenant age, the judgment Jesus had announced to that generation.

8. Hadrian and Syria Palaestina

The story did not stop in AD 70. A second revolt came under the Emperor Hadrian, known as the Bar Kokhba revolt (about AD 132-135). Its trigger was Hadrian’s plan to refound Jerusalem as a Roman colony, Aelia Capitolina, with a temple to Jupiter on the Temple Mount. Some ancient sources also point to an imperial ban on circumcision, though scholars debate that detail.

The revolt was led by Simon bar Kosiba, known as Bar Kokhba, “son of the star,” whom some in the movement, including the famous rabbi Akiva, hailed as Messiah. It achieved early success, but Rome crushed it at great cost. The Roman historian Cassius Dio claims hundreds of thousands died and that dozens of fortified towns and hundreds of villages were destroyed, though ancient casualty figures should be handled cautiously.

Notice also the Christian side. Justin Martyr, writing in the next generation, says Bar Kokhba punished Christians who refused to deny and blaspheme Jesus. The false messiah of that revolt demanded what the true Messiah’s followers would not give.

After the victory, Hadrian barred Jews from entering Aelia Capitolina, and the province of Judaea was renamed Syria Palaestina. The traditional date is around AD 135, and the renaming is widely understood as an effort to erase the connection between the Jews and the land. “Palaistine” was not new as a Greek word for the coastal region, since Herodotus used it centuries earlier, but the official provincial designation dates from this era. That name has described the region for nearly 1,900 years, approaching 2,000.

Eusebius records one more telling detail. The church in Jerusalem had been led by Jewish bishops until this time. After 135, its bishops were Gentile. The Jewish/Gentile question was already dissolving in the life of the church, and the Roman order had closed the old national framework.

9. There is no Jew or Gentile

The better covenant does not run on bloodlines. “There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus” (Gal 3:28). Christ “has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility... that he might create in himself one new man in place of the two, so making peace” (Eph 2:14-15). In the new self, “there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised... but Christ is all, and in all” (Col 3:11).

The kingdom has a Head, and the Head is the Last Adam (1 Cor 15:45). It has a new humanity, and that humanity is the New Creation: “If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come” (2 Cor 5:17).

There are no race qualifications for the kingdom. None qualifies you, and none disqualifies you. The first believers were Jews, thousands of them (Acts 2; 21:20), and the door opened immediately to the nations. Membership is by faith in the Last Adam, never by DNA.

10. Flesh and blood

Scripture keeps setting “flesh and blood” against the things of the kingdom.

  • Children of God are born “not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God” (John 1:13).
  • Peter’s confession of Christ was revealed not by “flesh and blood” but by the Father (Matt 16:17).
  • “Not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel... it is not the children of the flesh who are the children of God” (Rom 9:6-8).
  • “No one is a Jew who is merely one outwardly, nor is circumcision outward and physical” (Rom 2:28).
  • Paul, who had every fleshly credential, counted it loss: “We put no confidence in the flesh... circumcised on the eighth day, of the people of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin” (Phil 3:3-7).
  • And, “flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God” (1 Cor 15:50).

Read in covenant terms, “flesh and blood” points to the markers of the old order: genealogy and land. Bloodline and territory were the badges of the first covenant, and they do not carry into the kingdom. Paul even reads the two covenants as two mothers: Hagar, the present Jerusalem in slavery, and Sarah, the Jerusalem above, who is free and “our mother” (Gal 4:24-26).

11. The better covenant: better resurrection, and Jesus with us always

Hebrews keeps using one word: better. A better covenant on better promises (8:6), better hope (7:19), better sacrifice (9:23), and a better resurrection (11:35). Under the old order, access to God was mediated and restricted, through temple, priest, and veil. Under the new, Jesus promised continuous presence: the Spirit abides with and in believers forever, and the Father and Son make their home with those who keep his word (John 14:16-17, 23). Not occasional visitation in a building, but permanent indwelling.

The better resurrection is the superior, enduring life in Christ, already entered by those in the Last Adam (Rom 6:4-11; Col 3:1-4). It is contrasted with the temporary resuscitations of the old order. Physical mortality continues as part of created human nature, but covenantal alienation and condemnation have been dealt with. And believers have received “a kingdom that cannot be shaken” (Heb 12:28).

12. No ongoing condemnation

The judgment of AD 70 fell on that generation, and the execution of Jesus belongs to the same historical moment. Roman authority carried it out with the cooperation of the Jewish leadership of the time. That judgment is finished history. It is not a curse suspended over every Jewish person afterward.

“There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus” (Rom 8:1). Jewish people today stand under the same gospel as everyone else: justified by faith, not under residual guilt for the decisions of leaders two thousand years ago. Rome’s role is likewise historical, and the empire itself has passed. No people carries an inherited verdict.

13. What follows for the land

This is the point where the whole narrative lands. The land was part of the Old Covenant package: conditional, tied to the temple order, and held under the sanctions of Deuteronomy 28. The kingdom was taken from the tenants who killed the Son and given to a people producing its fruit (Matt 21:43). The house was left desolate (Matt 23:38). The age ended in AD 70.

The promises were made “to Abraham and to his offspring... meaning one, who is Christ” (Gal 3:16). The inheritance of the kingdom is not a real-estate claim based on genealogy. It is the unshakable kingdom already received by those in the Last Adam. The name Syria Palaestina, nearly two thousand years old, simply marks how long the old national order has been closed. The better covenant does not reopen it.
