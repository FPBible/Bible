Unmasking the “Devil”: Slanderer, Accuser, and the Pastoral Epistles

A unified commentary for the Fulfilled Prophecy Bible

Throughout the letters to Timothy, Paul uses the Greek words διάβολος (diabolos) and σατανᾶς (satanas). Later English translations capitalized these words and treated them as proper names for a cosmic fallen being. Recovering the original language and the first-century setting shows a far more practical and pastoral reality.

Recovering the Original Words

Διάβολος is an adjective meaning “slanderous” or “false-accusing.” It comes from the verb διαβάλλω — “to throw across,” “to set at variance,” “to accuse falsely.” As a noun it simply means “a slanderer” or “a false accuser.”

Σατανᾶς is the Greek form of the Hebrew שָׂטָן (satan), which means “adversary” or “opponent.” Neither word is a proper name in the original languages. They are functional descriptions.

Greek manuscripts of the New Testament do not capitalize these words as modern English Bibles do. The early uncial manuscripts are written entirely in capital letters; later minuscules do not mark “proper names” the way English convention requires. Capitalization of “Satan” and “Devil” is therefore an interpretive decision imposed by later translators, not a feature of the text Paul wrote.

How the Pastoral Epistles Themselves Use the Words

Paul uses diabolos in two distinct ways in the letters to Timothy:

1. As a clear description of human behavior

  • 1 Timothy 3:11 — women are not to be διάβολοι = “slanderers.”
  • 2 Timothy 3:3 — people in the last days will be διάβολοι = “slanderers.”
  • Titus 2:3 (same cluster of letters) — older women are not to be διάβολοι.

In these verses almost every modern translation correctly renders the word as “slanderers” or “malicious gossips.” No one capitalizes it as “Devils.”

2. With the definite article in the same letters

  • 1 Timothy 3:6 — “the condemnation of the διάβολος”
  • 1 Timothy 3:7 — “the snare of the διάβολος”
  • 2 Timothy 2:26 — “the snare of the διάβολος”

The same author, in the same letters, uses the identical word both for ordinary human slanderers and for “the διάβολος.” The most natural reading is that Paul is still speaking of the principle of false accusation and its human agents — the hostile critics and religious opponents who were actively slandering the gospel in the closing generation of the Old Covenant age.

The two occurrences of satanas in 1 Timothy follow the same pattern:

  • 1 Timothy 1:20 — Hymenaeus and Alexander are “handed over to Satan.” This is a recognized rabbinic-style idiom for putting someone outside the protection of the covenant community, leaving them to the consequences of the unbelieving world and its hostile powers.
  • 1 Timothy 5:15 — some younger widows have “turned aside after Satan.” In context this means they have abandoned their responsibilities and aligned themselves with the very critics and speculative teachers who were opposing the apostolic message.

Nothing in the Pastoral Epistles requires a cosmic fallen angel. Everything fits the first-century situation of human adversaries (both Jewish and Roman) who functioned as slanderers and opponents of the fledgling assemblies.

How Tradition Turned Common Nouns into a Proper Name

The shift from “slanderer / adversary” to a capitalized proper name for a dualistic cosmic rival did not arise from the Greek text itself. It developed through a series of interpretive moves:

  • The Septuagint had already used διάβολος to translate the Hebrew satan in places such as Job and Zechariah. Later Christian readers treated those passages as referring to a single personal Devil rather than a heavenly functionary or human accuser.
  • Early Church Fathers (especially Origen in the third century) linked Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 to a pre-Adamic fall of a chief angel. This “New Biography of Satan” is not present in the New Testament itself; it was read into the text.
  • Jerome’s Latin Vulgate rendered διάβολος as diabolus. Once Latin became the dominant theological language of the West, the capitalized Diabolus / Satanas became the fixed form.
  • English translators from Wycliffe (1380s) onward inherited this tradition. The Geneva Bible, Bishops’ Bible, and King James Version consistently capitalized “Satan” and often “Devil,” treating the words as proper names. This capitalization reinforced the dualistic doctrine that had already become standard.

The result is that modern readers encounter “the Devil” and “Satan” as fixed proper names, even though the original Greek of 1 and 2 Timothy uses ordinary nouns that the same author also applies to human beings who spread false accusations.

Pastoral Reality, Not Cosmic Dualism

When the Pastoral Epistles are read with the original meanings restored, Paul’s warnings become intensely practical:

  • Leaders must not give human slanderers ammunition (the “snare of the διάβολος”).
  • Those who persist in destructive teaching are put outside the community’s protection (“handed over to Satan”).
  • Younger believers who abandon orderly life and begin circulating speculative or ascetic teachings are said to have “turned aside after Satan” — they have joined the side of the opposition.

The letters are pastoral defense of the gospel in a hostile first-century environment, not a treatise on a metaphysical dualism. The “slanderer” and the “adversary” are the carnal mind expressed through human opponents who rejected the Messiah and sought to undermine the assemblies before the Old Covenant order was finally removed in AD 70.

Conclusion

That is the plain sense of the words Paul actually wrote. The capitalized “Devil” and “Satan” of later tradition are interpretive additions that turned common Greek and Hebrew nouns into a proper name supporting a dualistic cosmology. In the Pastoral Epistles the terms function as descriptions of false accusation, opposition, and the human agents who embodied those realities in the closing generation of the old age. Recovering the original language returns the letters to their practical, pastoral force and keeps the focus where Paul placed it — on guarding the gospel against real, present adversaries until the covenant transition was complete.
