The Serpent, the Dust, and the New Creation

A unified commentary for the Fulfilled Prophecy Bible

From the opening chapters of Genesis, Scripture presents a single, coherent picture of the old Adamic order. The serpent, the dust, the carnal mind, and the lusts of the world are not isolated themes scattered across the canon. They form one connected reality: a deceptive, self-exalting, earth-bound mode of existence that entered the human story, found expression among people, and was ultimately judged and brought to an end in Christ.

The Serpent as Symbolic Representation

In Genesis 3 the serpent is introduced as more crafty than any other beast of the field the Lord God had made. It appears within the created order, not above or outside it. Throughout Scripture, animals regularly function as idioms for character and spiritual condition. Lions depict strength or destruction, wolves depict predators, sheep depict the people of God.

Jesus uses this same pattern of speech. He calls the religious leaders of His day serpents and a brood of vipers (Matthew 23:33). He is not describing literal reptiles but identifying a crafty, deceptive, poisonous character. The serpent of Genesis 3 works the same way: it is the biblical emblem of the deceptive principle — the pull toward independence from God, self-exaltation, and life lived from the lower nature. It is not a dualistic fallen-angel figure or an eternal rival power standing over against God. It is creation's own capacity for self-deception, personified.

Dust, the Carnal Mind, and the Lusts of the World

God curses the serpent with the words, "dust you shall eat all the days of your life" (Genesis 3:14). This ties the serpent directly to the condition of fallen humanity. Man was formed from the dust of the ground (Genesis 2:7), and after the fall he hears, "you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (Genesis 3:19). Paul later calls the first Adam "a man of dust," and says that those still in him bear the man of dust's image (1 Corinthians 15:47–49).

The serpent feeds on the dust — the earthly, lower nature of fallen humanity. Out of this dust-bound existence rises the carnal mind, the mindset set on the flesh, which is hostile to God and cannot please Him (Romans 8:5–8). From the carnal mind flow the three defining marks of the world system:

"For all that is in the world—the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life—is not from the Father but is from the world." (1 John 2:16)

These are not scattered temptations. They are the natural fruit of a serpent-fed, dust-oriented, carnal mode of life.

The Offspring of the Serpent Are Human

God declares, "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring; he shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel" (Genesis 3:15). The serpent's offspring are not a separate, non-human race. They are human beings who align themselves with the principle the serpent represents.

The New Testament confirms this reading throughout. Jesus names certain people a brood of vipers. He tells others, "You are of your father the devil, and your will is to do your father's desires" (John 8:44). John writes that it is plain who are the children of God and who are the children of the devil (1 John 3:10). The conflict is real — but it runs between two lines of humanity, not between mankind and a supernatural rival: those who walk in the serpent's principle, and those who belong to the promise of the woman's Seed.

The Head Is Crushed

The same verse that announces the conflict also announces its outcome. The Seed of the woman will bruise the serpent's head — a fatal blow to its authority — while the serpent will only bruise the heel. This is the first gospel promise in Scripture.

The heel is bruised at the cross, where Christ suffers and dies. The head is crushed through His finished work. The serpent principle's power to deceive, to accuse, and to hold humanity in a dusty, carnal existence is broken there. In the covenantal transition of AD 70, the old religious order that had become this principle's vehicle — the Old Covenant system, with its priesthood, temple, and law of sin and death — is publicly judged and brought to an end. The head-crushing accomplished at the cross reaches its full, historical outworking in the old order's removal.

New Creation and Walking by the Spirit

Because the head has been crushed, those in Christ are no longer defined by the man of dust. They are the New Creation, the one new man, bearing the image of the heavenly Man. The old serpent-fed mode has lost its dominion. On God's holy mountain the curse no longer has power to hurt or destroy; there is no longer any curse.

Overcoming, then, is not a matter of wrestling a hostile entity within. It is a matter of living from a victory already accomplished. The old identity was crucified with Christ. Believers set their minds on the Spirit and on things above. They walk by the Spirit and so do not carry out the flesh's desires. They put off the old self and put on the new self, created after the likeness of God.

When the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, or the pride of life rises up, it is recognized for what it is — the voice of an already-defeated mode of existence, not the threat of an undefeated power. The response is not warfare against a rival but a return to what is already true: standing in the identity of the heavenly Man and walking forward in the freedom of the New Creation.

Conclusion

The serpent of Genesis 3 is the symbolic representation of the deceptive principle that feeds on the dust of earthly existence, produces the carnal mind, and expresses itself in the lusts of the world. Its offspring are human beings who walk in that principle. From the beginning, God announced that its head would be crushed. In Christ that crushing took place, and in AD 70 its historical vehicle was removed. Those who belong to the Seed of the woman now live as New Creation people, free to walk by the Spirit, no longer ruled by the dust or by the serpent's way.

This is the unified testimony of Scripture.
