Acts 6

1. Immediate Context

Acts 6 presents the first significant organizational challenge and the subsequent legal confrontation of the expanding New Covenant community. As the "remnant" of Israel grows during the transition of ages, the Apostles establish a division of labor to ensure the purity of the Word. The focus then shifts to Stephen, whose ministry of "signs and wonders" triggers a violent reaction from the diaspora synagogue, leading to formal charges that Jesus intended to destroy the Temple and change the customs of Moses—an accusation that correctly identified the covenantal upheaval of the first century.

2. Scripture (WEB)

Source (verbatim): https://ebible.org/web/ACT06.htm

1 Now in those days, when the number of the disciples was multiplying, a complaint arose from the Hellenists against the Hebrews, because their widows were neglected in the daily service. 2 The twelve summoned the multitude of the disciples and said, “It is not appropriate for us to forsake the word of God and serve tables. 3 Therefore select from among you, brothers, seven men of good report, full of the Holy Spirit and of wisdom, whom we may appoint over this business. 4 But we will continue steadfastly in prayer and in the ministry of the word.”
5 These words pleased the whole multitude. They chose Stephen, a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit, Philip, Prochorus, Nicanor, Timon, Parmenas, and Nicolaus, a proselyte of Antioch; 6 whom they set before the apostles. When they had prayed, they laid their hands on them.
7 The word of God increased and the number of the disciples multiplied in Jerusalem exceedingly. A great company of the priests were obedient to the faith.
8 Stephen, full of faith and power, performed great wonders and signs among the people. 9 But some of those who were from the synagogue called “The Libertines,” and of the Cyrenians, of the Alexandrians, and of those of Cilicia and Asia arose, disputing with Stephen. 10 They weren’t able to withstand the wisdom and the Spirit by which he spoke. 11 Then they secretly induced men to say, “We have heard him speak blasphemous words against Moses and God.” 12 They stirred up the people, the elders, and the scribes, and came on him and seized him, and brought him into the council. 13 They set up false witnesses who said, “This man never stops speaking blasphemous words against this holy place and the law. 14 For we have heard him say that this Jesus of Nazareth will destroy this place, and will change the customs which Moses delivered to us.” 15 All who sat in the council, fastening their eyes on him, saw his face like it was the face of an angel.

3. Fulfillment Notes

  • Multiplying in Jerusalem (v. 7): The fulfillment of the "firstfruits" harvest within the holy city before its impending desolation in AD 70.
  • Priests Obedient to the Faith (v. 7): A massive covenantal shift, as those once tethered to the Levitical system recognized the arrival of the New High Priest.
  • Signs and Wonders (v. 8): Miraculous validation of the New Covenant message during the apostolic generation.
  • Change the Customs (v. 14): While meant as a false accusation, it accurately describes the "change of law" necessitated by the change of priesthood (Hebrews 7:12).

4. Annotations

  • Hellenists (v. 1): Greek-speaking Jews who had adopted Hellenistic culture, representing the expansion of the Gospel beyond local Judean boundaries.
  • Daily Service (v. 1): Greek diakonia; the practical administration of the community's resources.
  • Synagogue of the Libertines (v. 9): Likely a synagogue of "Freedmen" or former captives, representing the Diaspora's resistance to the New Covenant.
  • Holy Place (v. 13): A technical term for the Jerusalem Temple.
  • Face of an Angel (v. 15): A sign of divine favor and the "glory of the New Covenant" that outshines the glory of Moses (2 Corinthians 3).

5. Fulfillment Commentary

Managing the Growth of the New Covenant Household

The tension between the Hellenists and the Hebrews (v. 1) was the first internal pressure point for the new body. In a fulfilled eschatology framework, this demonstrates that the Kingdom was not a static institution but a living, growing reality. The Apostles’ refusal to "forsake the word of God" to "serve tables" (v. 2) establishes the priority of the transition: the proclamation of the New Covenant took precedence over the administration of Old Covenant physical relief. This maturation of the remnant was necessary to prepare the corporate body for its role as the New Temple once the physical building was removed.

The Transition of Ministry: The Appointment of the Seven

The selection of the Seven (v. 3-6) was a prototype of the New Covenant diaconate. By choosing men "full of the Holy Spirit and of wisdom," the Apostles were proving that even "secular" service in the Kingdom was governed by the Spirit. This administrative shift mirrors the Old Testament elders appointed by Moses (Numbers 11), but with a New Creation distinction: the Spirit was now the primary qualification for leadership rather than mere tribal lineage. This decentralization of authority prepared the church for its global expansion beyond the localized priesthood of Jerusalem.

The Conversion of the Priests: A Fatal Blow to the Old System

Verse 7 records one of the most significant events in the apostolic generation: "a great company of the priests were obedient to the faith." This was a covenantal earthquake. The very men who performed the daily sacrifices were now acknowledging that those sacrifices were "shadows" that had been fulfilled in Christ. This conversion represented the legal "hollowing out" of the Old Covenant system from the inside. When the priests left their posts to follow Jesus, the Old Temple began its final descent into obsolescence, even while its walls still stood.

Stephen's Sign-Gifts and the Conflict of Wisdom

Stephen is the first non-apostle recorded as performing "great wonders and signs" (v. 8). This shows that the power of the "age to come" was not restricted to the Twelve but was the inheritance of the entire Spirit-filled body. His confrontation with the Synagogue of the Freedmen (v. 9-10) highlights the intellectual and spiritual clash between the letter of the Law and the Spirit of the New Covenant. The inability of the opponents to "withstand the wisdom" by which he spoke fulfills Jesus' promise in Luke 21:15, proving that the Spirit of the Father was directing the defense of the New Creation.

The Legal Charge: Destroy the Place and Change the Customs

The core accusation against Stephen (v. 13-14) is eschatologically profound. The false witnesses claimed Stephen said Jesus would "destroy this place" and "change the customs which Moses delivered." While meant as a smear, this was essentially what Jesus *had* prophesied in the Olivet Discourse. The "change of customs" was the movement from the Old Covenant Law to the New Covenant Law of the Spirit. The destruction of "this place" (the Temple) was the judicial climax of the generation. Stephen was not blaspheming; he was announcing the fulfillment of the Law and the arrival of the New Creation.

The Angelic Witness and the Glory of the New Covenant

The chapter ends with the council seeing Stephen’s face "like the face of an angel" (v. 15). This is a direct callback to Moses coming down from Sinai with a shining face. However, while Moses had to veil his face because the glory was "fading" (2 Corinthians 3:7), Stephen stands in the council with an unveiled, angelic radiance. This was God’s visual testimony to the Sanhedrin: the glory had moved from Moses to Christ, and from the Temple of stone to the Temple of the Spirit. Stephen was the living evidence that the New Creation had legally superseded the Old.

8. New Creation Reality

In Acts 6, we see that the internal growth and external opposition of the church are both evidence of the Spirit's active governance. We are not called to preserve "customs" for the sake of tradition, but to follow the living "Word of God" that transforms how we serve one another. Because the 'customs' have been changed and the Temple has been destroyed, we live in the permanent reality of the Spirit, where every believer is a priest and every act of service is a spiritual sacrifice in the New Creation.

9. Reflection

If the accusation against Stephen was that Jesus would "change the customs," in what areas of your own life are you still holding onto religious "customs" that might be obscuring the fresh life of the Spirit?
