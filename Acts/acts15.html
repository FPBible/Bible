Acts 15

1. Immediate Context

Acts 15 records the most important theological decision of the transition generation. Men from Judea come to Antioch teaching that Gentile believers must be circumcised according to the custom of Moses in order to be saved. Paul and Barnabas strongly disagree. The question is taken to the apostles and elders in Jerusalem. After much discussion, Peter reminds the council that God already chose that the nations would hear the gospel through his mouth, and that God made no distinction, cleansing their hearts by faith. Paul and Barnabas report the signs and wonders God has done among the Gentiles. James then cites the prophets (Amos 9) concerning the rebuilding of David’s tent so that the rest of mankind and all the Gentiles called by God’s name may seek the Lord. The judgment is clear: do not trouble the Gentiles who turn to God. A letter is sent affirming freedom from the yoke of the Law while asking only for a few practical abstentions. The door of faith to the nations remains open. This decision secures the New Covenant reality of one new man in Christ while the Temple still stands.

2. Scripture (WEB)

1 Some men came down from Judea and taught the brothers, “Unless you are circumcised after the custom of Moses, you can’t be saved.” 2 Therefore when Paul and Barnabas had no small discord and discussion with them, they appointed Paul, Barnabas, and some others of them to go up to Jerusalem to the apostles and elders about this question. 3 They, being sent on their way by the assembly, passed through both Phoenicia and Samaria, declaring the conversion of the Gentiles. They caused great joy to all the brothers. 4 When they had come to Jerusalem, they were received by the assembly and the apostles and the elders, and they reported everything that God had done with them.

5 But some of the sect of the Pharisees who believed rose up, saying, “It is necessary to circumcise them, and to command them to keep the law of Moses.” 6 The apostles and the elders were gathered together to see about this matter. 7 When there had been much discussion, Peter rose up and said to them, “Brothers, you know that a good while ago God made a choice among you that by my mouth the nations should hear the word of the Good News and believe. 8 God, who knows the heart, testified about them, giving them the Holy Spirit, just like he did to us. 9 He made no distinction between us and them, cleansing their hearts by faith. 10 Now therefore why do you tempt God, that you should put a yoke on the neck of the disciples which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear? 11 But we believe that we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they are.”

12 All the multitude kept silence, and they listened to Barnabas and Paul reporting what signs and wonders God had done among the nations through them. 13 After they were silent, James answered, “Brothers, listen to me. 14 Simeon has reported how God first visited the nations to take out of them a people for his name. 15 This agrees with the words of the prophets. As it is written, 16 ‘After these things I will return. I will again build the tabernacle of David, which has fallen. I will again build its ruins. I will set it up 17 that the rest of men may seek after the Lord: all the Gentiles who are called by my name, says the Lord, who does all these things.’ 18 “All of God’s works are known to him from eternity. 19 Therefore my judgment is that we don’t trouble those from among the Gentiles who turn to God, 20 but that we write to them that they abstain from the pollution of idols, from sexual immorality, from what is strangled, and from blood. 21 For Moses from generations of old has in every city those who preach him, being read in the synagogues every Sabbath.”

22 Then it seemed good to the apostles and the elders, with the whole assembly, to choose men out of their company, and send them to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas: Judas called Barsabbas, and Silas, chief men among the brothers. 23 They wrote these things by their hand:

“The apostles, the elders, and the brothers, to the brothers who are of the Gentiles in Antioch, Syria, and Cilicia: greetings. 24 Because we have heard that some who went out from us have troubled you with words, unsettling your souls, saying, ‘You must be circumcised and keep the law,’ to whom we gave no commandment; 25 it seemed good to us, having come to one accord, to choose out men and send them to you with our beloved Barnabas and Paul, 26 men who have risked their lives for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ. 27 We have sent therefore Judas and Silas, who themselves will also tell you the same things by word of mouth. 28 For it seemed good to the Holy Spirit, and to us, to lay no greater burden on you than these necessary things: 29 that you abstain from things sacrificed to idols, from blood, from things strangled, and from sexual immorality. If you keep yourselves from these, it will be well with you. Farewell.”

30 So, when they were sent off, they came to Antioch. Having gathered the multitude together, they delivered the letter. 31 When they had read it, they rejoiced over the encouragement. 32 Judas and Silas, also being prophets themselves, encouraged the brothers with many words and strengthened them. 33 After they had spent some time there, they were dismissed in peace from the brothers to the apostles. 35 But Paul and Barnabas stayed in Antioch, teaching and preaching the word of the Lord, with many others also.

36 After some days Paul said to Barnabas, “Let’s return now and visit our brothers in every city in which we proclaimed the word of the Lord, to see how they are doing.” 37 Barnabas planned to take John, who was called Mark, with them also. 38 But Paul didn’t think that it was a good idea to take with them someone who had withdrawn from them in Pamphylia, and didn’t go with them to do the work. 39 Then the contention grew so sharp that they separated from each other. Barnabas took Mark with him and sailed away to Cyprus, 40 but Paul chose Silas and went out, being commended by the brothers to the grace of God. 41 He went through Syria and Cilicia, strengthening the assemblies.

3. Fulfillment Notes

  • “He made no distinction… cleansing their hearts by faith” (v. 9): Peter’s decisive testimony. God has already shown that faith, not circumcision or Law-keeping, is the means of cleansing.
  • “We are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they are” (v. 11): The same grace for Jew and Gentile. There is one way of salvation for both.
  • The rebuilding of David’s tent (vv. 16–17): James cites Amos 9 to show that the inclusion of the Gentiles is the prophetic fulfillment of God restoring the Davidic house so that the nations may seek the Lord.
  • “It seemed good to the Holy Spirit, and to us” (v. 28): The council’s decision is presented as Spirit-guided. The burden of the Law is not to be laid on the Gentile disciples.
  • No greater burden than these necessary things (v. 28–29): Freedom from circumcision and the Mosaic Law is affirmed. The few practical requests are for the sake of table fellowship and purity in a mixed community, not for salvation.

4. Annotations

  • Sect of the Pharisees who believed (v. 5): Believing Pharisees who still insisted that Gentile converts must come under the full yoke of the Law. The issue is not unbelief but a restricted view of the New Covenant.
  • Yoke… which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear (v. 10): Peter acknowledges the historical burden of the Law. To impose it on Gentiles is to test God.
  • James and the tabernacle of David (vv. 13–18): James (the Lord’s brother) interprets the Gentile inclusion as the fulfillment of Amos 9. The restored “tent of David” is the Messianic reign that gathers the nations.
  • The four abstentions (v. 20, 29): Pollution of idols, sexual immorality, what is strangled, and blood. These address the most common pagan practices that would hinder table fellowship with Jewish believers and maintain basic holiness.
  • Sharp contention over Mark (vv. 37–39): Even after theological unity is reached, practical disagreement leads to separation. God uses both teams: Barnabas with Mark to Cyprus, Paul with Silas through Syria and Cilicia.

5. Fulfillment Commentary

The Crisis of the Yoke

The men from Judea bring a clear message: unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved. This is not a minor dispute about secondary practices. It strikes at the heart of the gospel. If circumcision and Law-keeping are required for salvation, then the cross is insufficient and the Gentile mission is only a half-measure. Paul and Barnabas recognize the danger and the matter is brought to Jerusalem.

Peter’s Testimony

Peter rises and reminds the council of what God already did through him with Cornelius. God made a choice that by Peter’s mouth the nations would hear the gospel and believe. God, who knows the heart, testified by giving them the Holy Spirit just as He did to the Jewish believers. He made no distinction. He cleansed their hearts by faith. Therefore, to put the yoke of the Law on the necks of these disciples is to test God. Peter’s conclusion is absolute: “We believe that we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they are.” There is one salvation for both.

James and the Prophets

James brings the discussion to its theological climax by citing Amos 9. The rebuilding of the fallen tent of David is not a future national restoration of Israel in the old covenant sense; it is the present Messianic work of God taking from the nations a people for His name. The prophets already foresaw that the restoration of David’s house would result in the Gentiles seeking the Lord. James’s judgment follows: do not trouble those from among the Gentiles who turn to God. The council agrees.

The Letter of Freedom

The letter sent to the Gentile churches is a charter of New Covenant freedom. It explicitly repudiates the teachers who had unsettled the souls of the believers. It affirms that the apostles gave no such commandment. It states that it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to the council to lay no greater burden than a few necessary things. The rejoicing in Antioch when the letter is read shows how heavy the threatened yoke had been. Freedom is confirmed.

Transition Generation Significance

Acts 15 is the decisive moment when the Jerusalem church publicly recognizes that the New Covenant people of God includes Gentiles without the requirements of the old covenant. Circumcision and the Law of Moses are not the boundary markers of the people of God. Faith and the gift of the Spirit are. This decision is made while the Temple still stands and the old order still has a generation left. The one new man is now officially acknowledged by the mother church. The door of faith to the nations remains open, and the gospel can continue to the ends of the earth without the burden of the Law.

8. New Creation Reality

Acts 15 secures the New Creation reality of one people of God defined by faith and the Spirit, not by circumcision or the Law of Moses. The council does not invent a new religion; it recognizes what God has already done. He has cleansed Gentile hearts by faith. He has given them the same Spirit. He has taken from the nations a people for His name in fulfillment of the prophets. The yoke that neither the fathers nor the present generation could bear is not to be placed on the necks of the new disciples. Freedom is declared. The one new man is acknowledged. And all of this is settled in Jerusalem while the Temple still stands, so that the gospel may go freely to the ends of the earth before the old covenant age ends.

9. Reflection

Peter asked why they would put a yoke on the disciples that neither the fathers nor they themselves had been able to bear. Are there still ways in which we are tempted to add extra requirements to the free grace of the Lord Jesus for full acceptance among God’s people? How does the council’s clear affirmation of salvation by grace through faith challenge any remaining tendency to measure spiritual status by external markers?
