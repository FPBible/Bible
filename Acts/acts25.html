Acts 25

1. Immediate Context

Acts 25 records the transition from Felix to Festus and Paul’s formal appeal to Caesar. Three days after arriving in the province, Festus goes up to Jerusalem. The chief priests and principal men of the Jews inform him against Paul and ask as a favor that Paul be summoned to Jerusalem — plotting to kill him on the way. Festus refuses and tells them to come down to Caesarea and accuse him there. After more than ten days he returns, sits on the judgment seat, and brings Paul out. The Jews from Jerusalem bring many serious charges they cannot prove. Paul answers that he has committed no offense against the law of the Jews, against the temple, or against Caesar. Festus, wishing to gain favor with the Jews, asks Paul if he is willing to go up to Jerusalem and be tried there. Paul replies that he is standing before Caesar’s judgment seat, where he ought to be tried. If he has done anything worthy of death he does not refuse to die, but if the charges are false no one can hand him over to them. He appeals to Caesar. Festus confers with his council and declares, “You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you shall go.” Some days later King Agrippa II and Bernice arrive in Caesarea. Festus lays Paul’s case before the king, admitting that the charges concerned questions of their own religion and about one Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul affirmed to be alive. He is perplexed how to investigate such matters and has nothing definite to write to the emperor. Agrippa expresses interest in hearing the man himself. The next day, with great pomp, Agrippa and Bernice enter the audience hall with the commanders and leading men of the city. Paul is brought in. Festus explains the situation and asks for help in formulating charges. The stage is set for Paul’s defense before Agrippa. The Lord’s promise that Paul will testify in Rome continues to move forward while the Temple still stands.

2. Scripture (WEB)

1 Festus therefore, having come into the province, after three days went up to Jerusalem from Caesarea. 2 Then the high priest and the principal men of the Jews informed him against Paul, and they begged him, 3 asking a favor against him, that he would summon him to Jerusalem, plotting to kill him on the way. 4 However Festus answered that Paul should be kept in custody at Caesarea, and that he himself was about to depart shortly. 5 “Let them therefore”, he said, “that are in power among you go down with me, and if there is anything wrong in the man, let them accuse him.”

6 When he had stayed among them more than ten days, he went down to Caesarea, and on the next day he sat on the judgment seat, and commanded Paul to be brought. 7 When he had come, the Jews who had come down from Jerusalem stood around him, bringing against him many and grievous charges which they could not prove, 8 while he said in his defense, “Neither against the law of the Jews, nor against the temple, nor against Caesar, have I sinned at all.”

9 But Festus, desiring to gain favor with the Jews, answered Paul and said, “Are you willing to go up to Jerusalem and be judged by me there concerning these things?”

10 But Paul said, “I am standing before Caesar’s judgment seat, where I ought to be tried. I have done no wrong to the Jews, as you also know very well. 11 For if I have done wrong and have committed anything worthy of death, I don’t refuse to die; but if none of those things is true that they accuse me of, no one can give me up to them. I appeal to Caesar!”

12 Then Festus, when he had conferred with the council, answered, “You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you shall go.”

13 Now when some days had passed, King Agrippa and Bernice arrived at Caesarea and greeted Festus. 14 As he stayed there many days, Festus laid Paul’s case before the king, saying, “There is a certain man left a prisoner by Felix; 15 about whom, when I was at Jerusalem, the chief priests and the elders of the Jews informed me, asking for a sentence against him. 16 I answered them that it is not the custom of the Romans to give up any man to destruction before the accused has met the accusers face to face and has had opportunity to make his defense concerning the matter laid against him. 17 When therefore they had come together here, I didn’t delay, but on the next day sat on the judgment seat and commanded the man to be brought. 18 When the accusers stood up, they brought no charges against him of such things as I supposed; 19 but had certain questions against him about their own religion and about one Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul affirmed to be alive. 20 Being perplexed how to inquire concerning these things, I asked whether he was willing to go to Jerusalem and there be judged concerning these matters. 21 But when Paul had appealed to be kept for the decision of the emperor, I commanded him to be kept until I could send him to Caesar.”

22 Agrippa said to Festus, “I also would like to hear the man myself.” “Tomorrow,” he said, “you shall hear him.”

23 So on the next day, when Agrippa and Bernice had come with great pomp, and they had entered into the place of hearing with the commanding officers and the principal men of the city, at the command of Festus, Paul was brought in. 24 Festus said, “King Agrippa, and all men who are here present with us, you see this man about whom all the multitude of the Jews petitioned me, both at Jerusalem and here, crying that he ought not to live any longer. 25 But when I found that he had committed nothing worthy of death, and as he himself appealed to the emperor, I determined to send him, 26 of whom I have no certain thing to write to my lord. Therefore I have brought him out before you, and especially before you, King Agrippa, that, after examination I may have something to write. 27 For it seems to me unreasonable, in sending a prisoner, not to also specify the charges against him.”

3. Fulfillment Notes

  • “I appeal to Caesar!” (v. 11): Paul’s decisive use of his Roman citizenship. The appeal both protects him from being handed over to the Jewish leaders and advances him toward the testimony in Rome that the Lord has promised.
  • “To Caesar you shall go” (v. 12): Festus’s formal acceptance of the appeal. The path to Rome is now legally fixed.
  • “One Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul affirmed to be alive” (v. 19): Festus’s summary of the real issue. Even a Roman governor recognizes that the dispute centers on the claim that Jesus is alive.
  • Nothing worthy of death (v. 25): Festus openly states that he has found no capital offense. The repeated Roman conclusion is that Paul has done nothing deserving death.
  • No certain thing to write (v. 26): The legal awkwardness of sending a prisoner to the emperor without clear charges. Agrippa’s hearing is needed to help formulate the report.

4. Annotations

  • Festus (v. 1): Porcius Festus, the new procurator of Judea (c. AD 59–62). He inherits the unresolved case of Paul from Felix.
  • Plot to kill on the way (v. 3): The same murderous intent that had earlier produced the conspiracy of more than forty men. The request to transfer Paul to Jerusalem is a cover for assassination.
  • King Agrippa and Bernice (v. 13): Agrippa II, son of the Herod of Acts 12, last of the Herodian rulers, with authority over the temple and the appointment of high priests. Bernice is his sister (and, according to contemporary rumor, more than sister).
  • Great pomp (v. 23): The formal entry of Agrippa and Bernice with military commanders and the leading men of the city underscores the public, high-status setting of the hearing that follows.
  • “My lord” (v. 26): The emperor (Nero at this time). Festus must send a report with the prisoner and has nothing substantive to write.

5. Fulfillment Commentary

A New Governor and the Same Plot

Festus arrives and almost immediately faces pressure from the Jewish leadership. They inform him against Paul and ask as a favor that the prisoner be brought to Jerusalem. The request is not for justice but for an opportunity to kill him on the road. Festus refuses the transfer and insists that the accusers come down to Caesarea. After more than ten days he returns, takes his seat on the judgment seat, and has Paul brought in. The charges are many and serious, yet they cannot be proved. Paul’s defense is simple and comprehensive: he has committed no offense against the Jewish law, against the temple, or against Caesar.

The Appeal

Festus, wishing to gain favor with the Jews, asks Paul if he is willing to go up to Jerusalem and be tried there before him. Paul recognizes the danger. He is already standing before Caesar’s judgment seat, where he ought to be tried. If he has done anything worthy of death he does not refuse to die; but if the accusations are false, no one has the right to hand him over to them. He therefore appeals to Caesar. Festus confers with his advisors and formally accepts the appeal: “You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you shall go.” The legal path to Rome is now set. The Lord’s promise that Paul will testify in Rome is being carried forward by the very legal mechanisms of the empire.

Festus and Agrippa

Some days later King Agrippa II and Bernice arrive in Caesarea and pay their respects to the new governor. Festus lays the case before the king. He recounts the request for a sentence against Paul, his own insistence on Roman procedure (the accused must face the accusers and have opportunity to defend himself), the hearing in Caesarea, and the surprising nature of the charges. He had expected political or criminal accusations; instead he found questions about their own religion and about one Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul affirmed to be alive. Perplexed how to investigate such matters, he had asked whether Paul was willing to go to Jerusalem. When Paul appealed to the emperor, Festus ordered him kept until he could be sent. Agrippa expresses interest in hearing the man himself. The next day is set.

The Formal Hearing

Agrippa and Bernice enter the audience hall with great pomp, accompanied by the military commanders and the leading men of the city. Paul is brought in. Festus addresses the assembly, explaining that the whole Jewish multitude has petitioned him that this man ought not to live any longer, yet he has found nothing worthy of death. Because Paul has appealed to the emperor, Festus has determined to send him. The problem is that he has nothing definite to write to the emperor. He has therefore brought Paul before them, and especially before Agrippa, so that after examination he may have something to write. It seems unreasonable to send a prisoner without specifying the charges. The stage is fully set for Paul’s defense before Agrippa in the next chapter.

Transition Generation Significance

Acts 25 advances the Lord’s promise that Paul will testify in Rome. The new governor faces the same murderous intent from the Jewish leadership. Paul’s appeal to Caesar both protects him from that intent and places him on the legal road to the capital. Festus himself summarizes the heart of the dispute: questions about their religion and about one Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul affirms to be alive. The resurrection remains the unproven, unrefuted center of the conflict. Roman officials repeatedly find no capital crime. The path to Rome continues to open while the Temple still stands and the old covenant structures still generate fierce opposition to the New Covenant witness.

8. New Creation Reality

Acts 25 shows the New Creation witness being carried forward by the legal structures of the empire itself. The appeal to Caesar both shields the apostle from the murderous intent of the old covenant leadership and places him on the road to the capital where the Lord has said he must testify. Even a Roman governor can summarize the heart of the matter: the dispute is about one Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul affirms to be alive. No capital crime is found. The path to Rome continues to open. The risen Lord’s promise is being kept while the Temple still stands and the opposition from the still-present old order remains fierce. The word of the resurrection advances under the protection of Caesar’s judgment seat.

9. Reflection

Paul’s appeal to Caesar was both a legal right and a strategic step that advanced the Lord’s promise of testimony in Rome. How do we discern when the use of legitimate legal or civic protections serves the advance of the gospel rather than a mere desire for personal safety? What does Festus’s repeated finding of “nothing worthy of death” teach us about the ultimate ground of the accusations against the Christian message?
