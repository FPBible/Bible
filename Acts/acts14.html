Acts 14

1. Immediate Context

Acts 14 continues the first missionary journey. In Iconium a great multitude of Jews and Greeks believe, but opposition forces Paul and Barnabas to flee to Lystra and Derbe. In Lystra a man lame from birth is healed. The pagan crowd, speaking in their own language, concludes that the gods have come down in human form and prepare to offer sacrifice. Paul and Barnabas tear their clothes and urgently redirect the people to the living God who made heaven and earth. Jews from Antioch and Iconium arrive, persuade the crowd, and stone Paul, leaving him for dead. He rises, continues to Derbe, makes many disciples, and then deliberately returns through the same cities, strengthening the new believers and appointing elders. They report back to Antioch that God has opened a door of faith to the nations. The pattern of the transition generation continues: the gospel advances among the Gentiles amid both miraculous power and fierce opposition, while the Temple still stands.

2. Scripture (WEB)

1 In Iconium, they entered together into the synagogue of the Jews, and so spoke that a great multitude both of Jews and of Greeks believed. 2 But the disbelieving Jews stirred up and embittered the souls of the Gentiles against the brothers. 3 Therefore they stayed there a long time, speaking boldly in the Lord, who testified to the word of his grace, granting signs and wonders to be done by their hands. 4 But the multitude of the city was divided. Part sided with the Jews and part with the apostles. 5 When some of both the Gentiles and the Jews, with their rulers, made a violent attempt to mistreat and stone them, 6 they became aware of it and fled to the cities of Lycaonia, Lystra, Derbe, and the surrounding region. 7 There they preached the Good News.

8 At Lystra a certain man sat, impotent in his feet, a cripple from his mother’s womb, who never had walked. 9 He was listening to Paul speaking, who, fastening eyes on him and seeing that he had faith to be made whole, 10 said with a loud voice, “Stand upright on your feet!” He leaped up and walked. 11 When the multitude saw what Paul had done, they lifted up their voice, saying in the language of Lycaonia, “The gods have come down to us in the likeness of men!” 12 They called Barnabas “Jupiter”, and Paul “Mercury”, because he was the chief speaker. 13 The priest of Jupiter, whose temple was in front of their city, brought oxen and garlands to the gates, and would have made a sacrifice along with the multitudes.

14 But when the apostles, Barnabas and Paul, heard of it, they tore their clothes and sprang into the multitude, crying out, 15 “Men, why are you doing these things? We also are men of the same nature as you, and bring you good news, that you should turn from these vain things to the living God, who made the sky, the earth, the sea, and all that is in them; 16 who in the generations gone by allowed all the nations to walk in their own ways. 17 Yet he didn’t leave himself without witness, in that he did good and gave you rains from the sky and fruitful seasons, filling our hearts with food and gladness.” 18 Even saying these things, they hardly stopped the multitudes from making a sacrifice to them.

19 But some Jews from Antioch and Iconium came there, and having persuaded the multitudes, they stoned Paul and dragged him out of the city, supposing that he was dead. 20 But as the disciples stood around him, he rose up, and entered into the city. On the next day he went out with Barnabas to Derbe.

21 When they had preached the Good News to that city and had made many disciples, they returned to Lystra, Iconium, and Antioch, 22 strengthening the souls of the disciples, exhorting them to continue in the faith, and that through many afflictions we must enter into God’s Kingdom. 23 When they had appointed elders for them in every assembly, and had prayed with fasting, they commended them to the Lord on whom they had believed.

24 They passed through Pisidia and came to Pamphylia. 25 When they had spoken the word in Perga, they went down to Attalia. 26 From there they sailed to Antioch, from where they had been committed to the grace of God for the work which they had fulfilled. 27 When they had arrived and had gathered the assembly together, they reported all the things that God had done with them, and that he had opened a door of faith to the nations. 28 They stayed there with the disciples for a long time.

3. Fulfillment Notes

  • Signs and wonders testify to the word of grace (v. 3): The Lord Himself confirms the message among both Jews and Greeks. Miracles serve the advance of the New Covenant word.
  • Turn from these vain things to the living God (v. 15): Paul and Barnabas refuse any glory and redirect pagan worshipers to the Creator. The gospel confronts idolatry at its root.
  • Through many afflictions we must enter into God’s Kingdom (v. 22): A clear statement of the cost of discipleship in the transition generation. Suffering is not an interruption but part of the path.
  • Appointed elders in every assembly (v. 23): The new Gentile churches are given local leadership and commended to the Lord. Structure serves the ongoing life of the one new man.
  • God has opened a door of faith to the nations (v. 27): The summary of the entire journey. The door is not forced open by human effort; God Himself has opened it.

4. Annotations

  • Iconium (v. 1): A significant city on the main route through southern Galatia. The pattern of synagogue preaching followed by mixed response continues.
  • Lystra (v. 8): A smaller Lycaonian town with a strong pagan culture. The healing of the lifelong cripple echoes the healing of the man at the Beautiful Gate (Acts 3).
  • Jupiter and Mercury (v. 12): Zeus and Hermes in Greek. Local legend held that these gods had once visited the region in human form and been refused hospitality. The crowd does not want to make the same mistake.
  • Stoned and left for dead (v. 19): Paul later refers to this experience (2 Corinthians 11:25; possibly Galatians 6:17). The disciples gather around him; he rises and re-enters the city the next day.
  • Returned to Lystra, Iconium, and Antioch (v. 21): Deliberate pastoral care. They do not abandon the new believers but strengthen them and appoint elders before moving on.

5. Fulfillment Commentary

Power and Division

In Iconium the word is spoken with boldness and confirmed by signs and wonders. A great multitude of both Jews and Greeks believe. Yet the city is divided. The pattern established in Pisidian Antioch continues: the gospel creates faith in some and fierce opposition in others. The apostles stay as long as possible, then move on when violence is imminent. The advance of the New Covenant word is never smooth or uncontested.

The Living God Versus the Gods of the Nations

The healing in Lystra produces an immediate pagan response. The crowd, speaking in their own language, concludes that the gods have come down. They prepare sacrifice. Paul and Barnabas react with horror, tearing their clothes. They insist they are only men of the same nature and call the people to turn from these vain things to the living God who made the sky, the earth, the sea, and all that is in them. Even in a thoroughly pagan setting, the apostles begin with creation and God’s general witness in rain and fruitful seasons. The gospel does not leave the nations in their idolatry; it calls them to the one true God.

Stoning and Rising

The same crowd that wanted to sacrifice to Paul soon stones him and drags him outside the city, thinking he is dead. The disciples stand around him. He rises and goes back into the city. The next day he continues the journey. This is not merely physical resilience; it is a living picture of the pattern Jesus Himself walked — rejection, suffering, and continued mission. The servant is not greater than his Master. In the transition generation the messengers of the New Covenant share in the afflictions of the Messiah.

Strengthening and Appointing

After making many disciples in Derbe, Paul and Barnabas deliberately return through the same cities where they had been opposed and nearly killed. They strengthen the souls of the disciples, exhort them to continue in the faith, and warn them that through many afflictions we must enter into God’s Kingdom. They appoint elders in every assembly, pray with fasting, and commend the new believers to the Lord. The work is not only proclamation; it is the forming of stable, locally led communities that can endure.

The Door of Faith to the Nations

When they return to the sending church in Syrian Antioch, they report all that God had done with them and that He had opened a door of faith to the nations. The summary is theological: God Himself has opened the door. The first missionary journey is presented as the beginning of a larger movement that will continue until the fullness of the Gentiles comes in — all while the old covenant age is still running its course.

8. New Creation Reality

Acts 14 shows the New Creation mission in its early, rugged form. The living God is proclaimed to people who have never known Him. Idols are refused. Miracles confirm the word. Opposition rises to the point of stoning. Yet the apostles rise, return, strengthen the new disciples, and appoint local elders. When they report back, the emphasis is not on their endurance but on what God has done: He has opened a door of faith to the nations. The one new man is being formed among the Gentiles through many afflictions, and the door remains open. This is the life of the transition generation — and the pattern for the ongoing mission of the church.

9. Reflection

Paul and Barnabas returned to the very cities where they had been rejected and nearly killed in order to strengthen the new believers. What does this teach us about pastoral courage and commitment? How does the statement “through many afflictions we must enter into God’s Kingdom” shape your expectation of the Christian life?
