Satan — The Covenantal Accuser

“Satan” is a functional title, adversary or accuser, not a personal name for a rival power. Within the fulfilled-eschatology framework, the Bible’s accuser language describes the condemning function of the Old Covenant order, which was brought to its end in the judgment culminating in AD 70.

Plain

The Hebrew word śāṭān means adversary or accuser. It belongs to legal and covenantal settings: bringing a charge, challenging standing, pressing a case.

Jesus named this mechanism Himself when He told the religious leaders that Moses was the one accusing them, the very Moses on whom they had set their hope (John 5:45). The Law was the accuser’s voice, and the Old Covenant administration of temple, priesthood and Law was where that voice was heard.

The New Testament personifies that order as “Satan” and “the devil,” in the same way Paul personifies Sin and Death as reigning kings (Romans 5:14). Personification does not make the opposition imaginary. The betrayal, slander and violence the first Christians suffered were real, and they came from identifiable first-century people and institutions.

Accusation is covenantal. When the Old Covenant reached its end, the system that sustained formal accusation lost its authority.

What the Word Means

  • Human adversaries. David is called a satan by the Philistine commanders (1 Samuel 29:4), and the LORD raises up a satan against Solomon (1 Kings 11:14).
  • An opposing messenger. The angel of the LORD stands in Balaam’s way as a satan (Numbers 22:22).
  • The accuser in a covenant courtroom. “The satan” appears as a prosecutor in Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3.

The meaning stays constant: opposition and accusation. What changes is who stands in the role. In Job and Zechariah the Hebrew carries the definite article, ha-śāṭān, marking a role rather than a personal name. In the New Testament the Greek ho Satanas and ho diabolos (“the slanderer”) carry that same courtroom sense into the first-century setting.

Job: The Accuser in a Judicial Scene

In Job 1–2 the sons of God present themselves, and the satan comes among them. The scene is a hearing: motives are questioned, integrity is tested, limits are set. The accuser acts under boundaries the LORD sets and at no point as an independent power.

Bounded, subordinate, functional. The accuser is a role within covenant administration, answerable to the LORD.

Read: Job 1–2 (WEB)

Zechariah 3: Accusation and Cleansing

Joshua the high priest stands in filthy garments, and the satan stands at his right hand to accuse. The high priest represents the nation, so his defilement exposes the whole covenant body, and the charge is legitimate under the Law.

The LORD rebukes the accuser, removes the filth and clothes Joshua in clean garments. The scene looks forward to the true High Priest, who is holy, innocent and unstained (Hebrews 7:26), and in whom the accuser finds nothing to charge.

Charge, defilement, rebuke, cleansing, restoration. This is covenant courtroom vocabulary.

Read: Zechariah 3; Hebrews 7:26 (WEB)

Genesis 3 and Revelation’s Imagery

Genesis presents a serpent who tempts. The text does not call it “the devil” or “the satan.” Revelation draws that connection when it describes the great dragon, the ancient serpent, called the devil and Satan (Revelation 12:9; 20:2).

Revelation’s dragon, serpent and beast carry the Genesis pattern of temptation and deception into the first-century conflict. They depict the persecuting order that opposed Christ and His church, and the book shows that order cast down and judged.

Read: Genesis 3; Revelation 12:9; 20:2 (WEB)

Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28: Taunt-Songs Against Kings

These chapters are often read as the origin story of a fallen angel. In context, Isaiah 14 is a taunt against the king of Babylon, and Ezekiel 28 is a lament over the king of Tyre. Both are prophetic mockery aimed at named human rulers.

In prophetic speech, rising to heaven and being cast down from it is the language of political exaltation and collapse. It describes a dynasty that exalted itself and was brought low, and Scripture does not attach that language to a pre-creation rebellion.

The Wilderness: The Last Adam Tested

After the Father declares Him Son at His baptism, Jesus is led into the wilderness for forty days, mirroring Israel’s forty years. Where the first Adam failed in a garden and Israel failed in the wilderness, the Last Adam stands.

Each temptation presses Him to step outside covenant trust: to secure provision apart from the Father, to force divine vindication by spectacle, to take authority before its appointed time. Jesus answers only from Deuteronomy (8:3; 6:16; 6:13), Israel’s own covenant document, and the accusation finds nothing to hold.

Scripture does not tell us in detail how the encounter was experienced, and the page need not decide that. What the text shows is that Sonship is questioned, obedience is tested, and the Law’s demands are met in full.

Read: Matthew 4; Luke 4; Deuteronomy 6–8 (WEB)

The Accuser in the Gospels and Acts

The leadership centred on the temple, including the chief priests, the Sanhedrin and the scribes, brought the charges against Jesus and later against His church. In the New Testament’s courtroom vocabulary of charge, testimony and condemnation, they act as the Law’s prosecutors.

This describes a first-century institution and its leaders. It makes no claim about the Jewish people. The first Christians were themselves Jews, and Paul laments the very leaders he once served alongside.

The gospel announces the decisive shift. Condemnation is removed, standing rests on Christ’s faithfulness, and the record of debt that stood against us is cancelled at the cross (Colossians 2:14). Hebrews adds that the old covenant was already growing obsolete and was about to vanish (Hebrews 8:13), which brings the story to AD 70.

The Apostolic Age: “Satan” in the Epistles

Between the cross (c. AD 30) and the fall of the temple (AD 70), the church lived in the overlap of the ages. The New Covenant was inaugurated, while the temple order and its political allies still resisted the gospel with force. That setting explains the apostolic warnings.

Handed over to Satan (1 Corinthians 5:5; 1 Timothy 1:20)

The language describes a judicial act of exclusion. The church was the New Covenant sanctuary, and to be put out of it was to be returned to the sphere of the adversary: the synagogue system that punished believers, or the pagan world. The aim was restoration, that the spirit might be saved.

The roaring lion (1 Peter 5:8)

The lion is Old Testament idiom for predatory rulers (Proverbs 28:15; Zephaniah 3:3). Peter writes to scattered, suffering believers, and the devil who prowls is the slandering, persecuting opposition that was hauling Christians before tribunals.

Rulers and authorities (Ephesians 6:11–12)

Paul’s “schemes of the devil” include the pressure to pull believers back under the Law’s condemnation. The rulers and authorities he names stood behind that pressure. The struggle was real, and it belonged to that age.

The snare of the devil (1 Timothy 3:7; 2 Timothy 2:26)

Diabolos means slanderer or false accuser. Outsiders were watching the church for any scandal that could be used against it, so an elder with a poor reputation handed accusers their ammunition.

Revelation: The Accuser Cast Down

Revelation 12:10 calls the devil “the accuser of our brothers,” using a Greek term for a prosecutor who brings formal charges (katēgōr, a form of katēgoros). The dragon persecutes the woman and her offspring, then is cast down, and a voice declares that the kingdom of our God and the authority of His Christ have come.

This was near for the first readers. Paul tells the Romans that the God of peace will crush Satan under their feet quickly (Romans 16:20), written around AD 57. The judgment imagery of the lake of fire and the second death belongs to the same horizon, the fall of Jerusalem and the end of the order that accused, persecuted and prosecuted under the Law.

Cast down. Dethroned. Judged. Revelation shows the persecuting order meeting its historical end.

Read: Revelation 12; 20; Romans 16:20 (WEB)

Fulfilled: The System Ended in AD 70

The temple fell, the priesthood ended, the sacrifices stopped and the legal structure that sustained formal covenant accusation was gone. With the Old Covenant administration ended, the accuser’s court had no jurisdiction.

No temple, no prosecutor. Accusation was a function of the old order, and it ended with that order.

The Serpent in the Dust (Isaiah 65:25)

Isaiah’s new creation has the wolf and the lamb feeding together, and the serpent’s food is dust. Eating dust is an old Near Eastern image of total subjugation (Psalm 72:9; Micah 7:17). The serpent appears in this vision as a defeated figure, and the new creation is secure.

What Now?

This changes how a believer lives, which is why it matters beyond the history.

  • You are not under covenant prosecution. The Law’s power to condemn was exhausted in Christ (1 Corinthians 15:56; Colossians 2:14).
  • You do not have to prove your sonship through spiritual performance or anxious self-monitoring.
  • You live under reconciliation, with clean standing, direct access and real peace.
No condemnation, no accuser. The new creation is the end of covenant prosecution as a governing reality. You stand in Christ.

Summary

  • Śāṭān means adversary or accuser, a role before it is a name.
  • In Job and Zechariah the accuser is a bounded courtroom figure under the LORD.
  • Genesis does not call the serpent the devil, and Revelation draws that connection as imagery.
  • Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 are taunt-songs against the kings of Babylon and Tyre.
  • The wilderness temptation is the covenant testing of the Last Adam.
  • The New Testament’s “Satan” and “devil” are the personified accusing, persecuting order of the first century.
  • Revelation shows that order cast down, and Romans 16:20 promised it quickly.
  • The accusing order ended in AD 70, and the believer stands in Christ with no condemnation.
